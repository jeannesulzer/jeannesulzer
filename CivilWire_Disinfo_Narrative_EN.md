# CivilWire and disinformation: an evidence argument

Draft narrative for #Disinfo2026, Vilnius, 6 to 8 October 2026
Jeanne Sulzer, Impact Litigation Lab

---

## The thesis in one paragraph

Disinformation is usually framed as a speech problem, and the responses follow:
moderation, labelling, fact-checking, platform regulation. A litigator sees a
different problem. What defeats a false account in a courtroom, before a treaty
body, in a sanctions file or in a universal jurisdiction case is not a rebuttal.
It is a document, attributable to a known author, fixed in time, that still
resolves years later. Civil society produces a very large share of that
documentation. It is also the layer of the record that is least findable, least
durable and least citable. CivilWire is addressed to that gap, and to nothing
else.

---

## Three failures, in the order they bite

### 1. Dispersion

The documentation exists. It is spread across several hundred organisational
websites, in a dozen languages, with no common point of entry, and it is
discoverable mostly through search engines that rank by popularity rather than
by provenance. A national organisation that has documented a pattern of
detention for fifteen years is, in practice, invisible beside an international
organisation that published once.

This is not a theoretical ranking problem. It determines which documentation
enters a brief, a submission or an article, and therefore which account of
events becomes the one on the record.

### 2. Perishability

Organisations close, and their archives close with them. The mechanisms are
well known to this audience: "foreign agent" legislation in Russia, extended in
2022 to anyone deemed under foreign influence; the Georgian transparency of
foreign influence law of 2024; the mass cancellation of NGO legal personality in
Nicaragua since 2018. Deregistration is followed by the loss of the bank
account, then the domain, then the archive.

Add ordinary link rot. The Harvard study by Zittrain, Albert and Lessig on
reference rot in legal citations found that roughly half the URLs cited in
United States Supreme Court opinions no longer pointed to the material cited,
and a considerably higher proportion in leading law journals. If that is true of
a Supreme Court footnote, it is true many times over of a footnote pointing to a
Syrian or Burmese documentation group.

*(Check the exact figures against the 2014 paper before quoting them from a

### 3. Non-citability

A URL is not a citation. It carries no commitment that the thing it points to
will still be there, and no way to show what was there when it was consulted.
The documentation that is hardest to replace is the documentation most likely to
be hosted on the most fragile infrastructure.

## The platform paradox, which belongs in this room

Counter-disinformation enforcement and evidence preservation pull against each
other, and the clearest demonstration is the Syrian material.

When YouTube deployed machine-learning classifiers against violent extremist
content in 2017, very large volumes of Syrian conflict footage were removed.
A significant part of it had been uploaded by documentation groups and local
media precisely so that it would exist. The Syrian Archive, now part of
Mnemonic, was built around this problem: to preserve, verify and make
retrievable material that platforms delete faster than anyone can archive it.

The lesson generalises. Any record that lives only on a platform is held at the
discretion of that platform's moderation policy, and that policy is set by the
disinformation debate itself. A record that does not live only on a platform is
a hedge against the enforcement we are all asking for.

## Where the law already points

Three reference points, all of which pre-date CivilWire and none of which it
claims to implement.

**The Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations** (Human Rights
Center, UC Berkeley School of Law, with the UN Office of the High Commissioner
for Human Rights, 2020, issued as a UN publication in 2022) is the standard that
sets out what open source material must satisfy to be usable: provenance,
preservation, documented handling, and the ability to show that an item is what
it is said to be. It is the vocabulary this field now shares.

**The ICC has already acted on open source material.** The arrest warrant issued
by Pre-Trial Chamber I against Mahmoud Al-Werfalli on 15 August 2017, for murder
as a war crime, rested substantially on video circulated on social media, with a
second warrant in July 2018. Whatever one thinks of the case's subsequent
history, the evidentiary proposition was settled there: material published
openly, by non-state actors, can carry an international arrest warrant.

**The standing mechanisms are built to receive civil society material.** The
International, Impartial and Independent Mechanism for Syria, created by General
Assembly resolution 71/248 of 21 December 2016, and the Independent
Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar, created by Human Rights Council resolution
39/2 of 27 September 2018, both exist to collect, consolidate and preserve
evidence, including from organisations that are not states. Their output depends
on an input that nobody is responsible for keeping.

## The regulatory asymmetry worth naming out loud

Article 40 of the Digital Services Act builds a route for vetted researchers to
reach platform data. It is a serious instrument and it took years.

There is no equivalent instrument, and no obligation on anyone, for the
documentary output of civil society itself. We have legislated access to what
platforms hold about speech, and left unaddressed the durability of the
documentation that contradicts it. That asymmetry is the space CivilWire sits
in, and it is a space that policy, not technology, left open.

## What CivilWire does, precisely

- **One chronological feed** of what human rights organisations publish:
  reports, statements, alerts. 377 organisations in 125 countries, six
  languages, right to left native.
- **Nothing is rewritten.** Every entry links to the document on the publisher's
  own site. No summaries, no editorial ranking, no algorithmic hierarchy. The
  order is time.
- **Each dispatch carries a permanent citation reference**, and a copy is
  deposited with the Internet Archive, so that a footnote still resolves when
  the publisher's site does not.
- **Inclusion is delegated.** An organisation is in the index because an
  accreditation network that publishes its own admission criteria has admitted
  it. The judgement about who counts as civil society is not mine. This is the
  property that makes the index usable as a research instrument rather than as a
  curated reading list.
- **Free, no account, non-profit**, published by Impact Litigation Lab.

## What CivilWire is not

This matters more than the feature list, because overclaiming in front of this
audience is fatal.

It is not a fact-checker, and it makes no assessment of the accuracy of what it
indexes. It is not an evidence repository and it maintains no chain of custody,
so nothing in it is admissible by virtue of being in it. It is not an archive of
primary material; it is an index of published documents, plus a capture of
record. It does not replace the Berkeley Protocol, the mechanisms, or Mnemonic.
It makes their input findable, and it makes a citation to that input survive.

---

## Closing, for a legal audience

The counter-disinformation field has spent a decade arguing about what should be
taken down. Comparatively little attention has gone to whether what contradicts
a false account will still be retrievable, and citable, in five years. Those are
evidentiary questions, and they are answered before any litigation begins, by
whether somebody kept the record findable.

CivilWire is a small answer to a narrow part of that. It is in beta, the
coverage is uneven, and the fastest way to improve it is to be told what is
missing.
